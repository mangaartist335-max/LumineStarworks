'Impact of Eden' Premiere Review: Burst Studio Announces Itself in 24 Minutes

Debut studio Burst opens its catalog with a fall premiere so dense it feels like a finale — and it works.
By Cassidy Renault-Hammond | Senior Anime Correspondent | October 6, 2013 — 4:18 PM EDT

Burst's debut anime Impact of Eden (2013) aired its first episode tonight on JP broadcast at 01:58 JST, and on the off chance you missed the chatter rolling through every anime forum on the internet for the last six hours: it landed. Hard.

With Kaihatsu Miura directing, Keito Kisaragi on series composition, and Hiroyuki Sawano composing the score, the premiere delivers the kind of front-loaded confidence that most studios save for episode three. It's not subtle about it. The cold open hits you with a date stamp from eleven years before the main timeline, the rooftop scene gives you the show's emotional thesis in ninety seconds, and the YAMAGA284 boss fight in the third act features sakuga choreography that would make a senior studio nervous about its season.

This is a debut studio. And let's be precise about that: not "Burst's first TV series." Burst's first piece of animation, full stop. No early ONA. No warm-up short. No web pilot. No movie before the flagship. Burst has no prior credits to point at. Impact of Eden is the studio's birth certificate. And they came out swinging like a finale.

What's actually in the episode

The setup, for the spoiler-cautious: After struggling at Maple Grove City with his parents, twelve-year-old Sora Gauthier decides to head out on a Soul Anchor Journey to avenge his mother's survival, joining forces with allies Abigail June, Grayson Rothenberg, and Ben Smythe inside Planetation. Running the system from above, however, is a council called Student Council 1, led by its president, Miyuki Kagemitsu. Over a single school day and an evening match, the episode establishes the show's "Burst Step" combat language and culminates in a boss encounter against an enemy player called YAMAGA284.

What sets the premiere apart isn't the premise — VR game anime has been a well-worn genre since 2002. What sets it apart is the pace and the specificity. Each character introduction is a working scene, not an info dump. Each chunk of world-building is delivered through action, not exposition. And the episode finds time, somehow, for full-length business operations by Gamlet (Kishou Taniyama) and Nova (Yui Ishikawa) and a quiet KO HQ scene where the show's adult-world stakes are conveyed entirely through both Nova and Gamlet's tone of voice.

"No fear, no hesitation, no regrets!
If the walls above stand in front of you, advance to crush them!
Restrictions behind history are merely an excuse to refuse living!
That is the real truth of humanity!
Because of our mistakes, we deserve a path to choose where we keep moving forward!
Therefore, our choice towards history is REFUSAL!"

That's Miyuki, delivered live in the WarWorld lobby, two minutes before her squad and Sora's squad walk into the YAMAGA fight. It's the most quotable line of the fall season so far, and the show puts it in episode one. Saori Hayami delivers it like she's been waiting all year to read it.

Burst earned the budget

The animation is the bigger story. Burst spun out of Flow Pictures' late-era cinematic unit — the same staff culture that produced the iconic Steelbound Testament — and that pedigree shows. The YAMAGA284 fight features heat distortion compositing on the Crucible Blades, individually-keyed multi-frame slow motion on the Burst Step animations, and silhouette-work during the final exchange that delivers high-quality fluid sakuga choreography.

But the small choices are the more impressive flex. Burst gave a full key-frame pass to a four-second comedic emote scene featuring Likko Valenne (Student Council 1's Secretary, voiced by Ayane Sakura), where five distinct NPCs animate copying her dance rhythm individually instead of looping a single crowd animation. That's labor a debut studio is not supposed to be able to afford. And they did it for a comedy beat.

The grapple-pistol finisher Abigail June lands on YAMAGA at the end of act three is the headline sakuga sequence and it deserves the attention. The cut is staged across roughly forty drawings inside a 1.4-second window, with three discrete physics passes layered into a single take: the recoil arc on Abigail's shoulder as the pistol fires, the cable's whip-lash counter-curve in mid-air, and the impact deformation on YAMAGA284's plating at the moment of contact. Each of those is the kind of pass that, on most TV productions, would get one of the three; Burst gave it all three, on a single cut, on a debut. That is the headline. But the headline is not the story. The story is the under-discussed flex: the deliberate, per-character animation work given to every female character in the cast, and the structural decision to put the show's biggest emotional, ideological, and combat beats in the hands of those women without exception.

Take the women one at a time. Miyuki Kagemitsu's stillness in the pre-Void Rift lobby is animated as a deliberate composition choice, not a budget save: she crosses her arms into a held pose, the OST cuts to silence underneath, and her squad in the background freezes their idle loops on the same beat â€” five separate keyed character poses holding at once, which is the opposite of how a TV anime would normally cheat that shot. Her micro-frown softening for a single frame at the close of "Therefore, our choice towards history is REFUSAL!" is the kind of animation choice you do not stumble into; somebody studied her. Echo Desmond's stylish dual-blade fight sequence is the louder flex: she threads between two YAMAGA units with a low sliding cut, reverses into a backhand parry, and uses the rebound to vault into a corkscrew slash without the camera ever losing her center of gravity. The sakuga is not just "fast"; it is readable speed â€” full-body anticipation before every blade contact, separate arcs for each weapon, hair and jacket lagging a few frames behind the turn, and red-orange impact frames that punctuate the hits without hiding the choreography. The staff is introducing her as a veteran combatant first and a visual second, and the camera language enforces it. Likko Valenne's chaotic comedy timing is the most expensive comedy beat on the episode: five distinct NPC dance loops keyed individually instead of cycled, plus an independent hair-physics pass that bobs at a different timing than her head movement, which is effectively giving her hair its own animator credit. Nova's posture-as-grief portrayal in the KO HQ scene is the most quietly virtuosic cut in the entire premiere â€” a one-to-two pixel shoulder drop when she looks at the player counter, drawn-in exhaustion held for fourteen frames with zero dialogue overlay, and a re-grounding of her stance on the line about Frontier Risk that asks the audience to read a working professional carrying institutional weight. Lisa and Minko's riot-read in the hallway scene gets walking-and-talking acting beats with shoulder turns and eyeline shifts that route the scene's information without pushing it into expository over-the-shoulder cuts. None of these women are framed as decoration. None of them are framed as reaction shots for a male lead. They are framed as the people delivering the scenes they are in.

The thematic implication is the part the discourse hasn't fully caught up with yet. The show's ideological spine — Miyuki's lobby speech — is delivered by a woman. The show's adult-world stakes — the Frontier Risk diagnosis at KO HQ — are delivered by a woman. The show's headline sakuga finisher on the episode's marquee fight is landed by a woman. The show's most expensive single comedy beat is built around a woman. The show's cleanest dual-blade speed-language showcase is given to a woman. That is not "male dominate, female support." That is a structural reversal: the women here are not adjacent to the lead role, they are the lead roles, plural. Burst's storyboard team, led by Tomoe Suzuhara, animated the women in this episode as characters first and as visuals second, and the writing under Keito Kisaragi's series composition makes that the show's organizing principle, not a one-episode flourish. That kind of parity-by-leadership treatment is not a given in 2013, and frankly it's not a given in 2026 either. Eden is doing it on a debut studio's first ep. That deserves to be said out loud.

And the boys, to be clear, are not getting shortchanged either. Sora's MetaCode arm-charge animates across three independent motion layers — skin deformation, hardlight glow, and the data-particle leak — over a single ~12-frame sequence; that's three animator passes for one cut, and it establishes the show's effects vocabulary right there. Grayson's Crucible Dual Blade choreography on YAMAGA284 carries heat-distortion compositing on every flaming arc, with hand-drawn 2-frame motion blur underneath the digital pass — that's labor, not a filter. Ben's NodeCore work — the drill spin-up and the gigantic-fist smash that lands square on YAMAGA284 — is a standout impact-frame sequence: weight-shift on the wind-up, a held single-frame impact pose at contact, then a multi-layer dust-and-shrapnel burst that sells the hit physically rather than as a stylized effect. Even Gamlet's quiet coffee scene with Nova rests on micro-acting in the shoulders. The point isn't that the women got more attention than the men. It's that nobody in this cast got decoration treatment. Burst gave parity. That, in 2013, is rarer than it should be.

And there's a second register of male animation work worth flagging, because the discourse keeps under-counting it. The clearest example is Sora's MetaCode mid-charge transformation in the YAMAGA fight — a four-second window where the data-particle leak escalates from a hairline glow to a full hardlight overlay across seven layered cuts, while Sora's face cycles through a keyed micro-expression on every pose: fear, then commitment, then a single-frame neutral that lands the swing. That is character animation grafted onto an effects sequence, not the other way around. Grayson's calibration-card beat earlier in the episode gets a held two-second quiet shot with the redacted KO-tier 3 tag flashing once on the card surface — the kind of background-information frame a debut studio is allowed to cheat and Burst chose not to. Ben's classroom-door-lock at the end of his hallway sprint, normally the kind of beat a TV production cycles a stock animation for, gets full hand-on-handle physics on the lock turn. And the supporting credited men — William Jones (Hiro Shimono), Kieran Atsushi (Hiroshi Kamiya), Michael Pentulengro (Yoshimasa Hosoya), Andreas Walker (Tasuku Hatanaka) — are not crowd-art either: each gets at least one keyed reaction shot or named acting beat, with cast-of-this-tier voice work underneath. On a debut studio's first episode in 2013, that's a resource-allocation flex on its own. The shape, when you sit with it, is that the men in Eden are animated as characters in motion — becoming, escalating, transforming — while the women are animated as characters who already are. Both registers are working in parallel, both registers are working hard, and Burst is asking the audience to read both at once.

Voice cast on a debut studio production

The vocal cast is, frankly, embarrassing for a debut studio. We have Mariya Ise as Sora Gauthier, Nobuhiko Okamoto as Grayson Rothenberg, Kana Hanazawa as Abigail June, Kaito Ishikawa as Ben Smythe, Saori Hayami as Miyuki, Rina Satou as Echo, Hiro Shimono as William, Hiroshi Kamiya as Kieran, Ayane Sakura as Likko, Yoshimasa Hosoya as Michael Pentulengro, and Tasuku Hatanaka as Andreas Walker. Yui Ishikawa, fresh off her widely-acclaimed work earlier this year, plays Nova. Kishou Taniyama plays Gamlet.

That cast, for a debut studio's first-ever animated production — period, no qualifier, no prior work for the producers to point at — is unheard of. Whoever did the producer relationships on this project knew exactly who they were calling and why.

The not-as-good

It's not flawless. One line of YAMAGA284 dialogue early in the fight is intentionally off-putting in a way that crosses into "uncomfortable for shock value" territory and we wish it had been written with more care, given the age of the squad. The hallway riot scene in act one uses a deliberately stiffer animation style that some viewers will read as budget-cutting rather than as a stylistic choice (it's a stylistic choice, but the show could have communicated that better). And the sheer density of the premiere will lose some viewers who wanted a slower introduction to the cast.

None of these are dealbreakers. But they're worth flagging.

What this means for the season

Burst just announced itself. Impact of Eden (2013) is the kind of episode-one that makes the rest of the fall season nervous, because if Burst can sustain even 75% of this density across a 25-episode run, this is going to be a Show of the Year conversation by January. The early YAL score (8.51 after a single episode and 2,847 votes as of this writing) suggests the audience already knows.

Episode two airs Sunday October 13 at 01:58 JST. We will be there.

VERDICT
8.5 / 10
A staggeringly confident debut. Burst handled the production logistics, Takuya's original concept delivers a layered cast, and the staff list reads like a who's-who. The single weak dialogue beat and a premiere this dense risking viewer fatigue hold us back from a 9. Everything else is a clinic in how to open a 25-episode show.

Cassidy Renault-Hammond covers animation production for AniWire. She has been writing about Japanese animation since 2008. Disclosure: AniWire received no press materials from Burst or AniBlex for this review; the review is based on the public Japanese broadcast.
